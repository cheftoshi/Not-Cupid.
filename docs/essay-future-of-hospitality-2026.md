# Run Clubs Are Eating the Restaurant Industry

*The table lost its monopoly on the evening. Here's where it went.*

*(Alternate titles, ranked: "Nobody Drinks. Everybody Runs." · "Last Call: The End of the Table's Monopoly on the Evening" · "The Third Place Sweats Now" · "Where Did Everybody Go?")*

On a Wednesday night in Manhattan, more than a thousand people gather to run three miles together. They are young, roughly half men and half women, and most of them are not there for the running. Lunge Run Club — like hundreds of clubs that have multiplied across American cities — ends at a bar, but the bar is the afterthought. The point was the miles, the sweat, the easy conversation that happens when you're moving next to a stranger instead of sitting across from one. A few blocks away, a fifteen-year-old restaurant is working its last service. The owner will tell the neighborhood blog what every closing owner now tells the neighborhood blog: the math stopped working.

We are living through the most confusing moment in the modern history of hospitality, because both of these things are true at once. Americans have never spent more on going out, and restaurants have never had a harder time surviving it. To understand why, you have to stop thinking of a restaurant as a place that sells food, and start thinking of it as what it always was: an environment constructed by social culture. Food and beverage is not the product. It is the medium. And the culture that shaped the medium has moved.

## The reckoning

The numbers describe something closer to a correction than a slump. Twenty-one restaurant chains filed for Chapter 11 in 2024 — the most since the pandemic year itself — and more than twenty followed in 2025, taking Red Lobster, TGI Fridays, and Hooters through the courts. The carnage is not just corporate: independent restaurants shrank 2.3 percent in 2025 while chains grew, and Black Box Intelligence estimates that nine percent of all American full-service restaurants are at risk of closure in 2026. Forty-two percent of operators say they were unprofitable last year. When costs have risen by nearly a third since 2019, losing even a portion of your peak traffic is fatal.

David Chang saw this coming before the data did. He has long described the 2010s restaurant boom as a bubble — "in some degree, it was a bubble for all the restaurants pre-pandemic" — an era when cheap money, Instagram, and a golden age of food media built more dining rooms than the culture could sustain. But his sharpest diagnosis came on the TBPN podcast late last year, and it wasn't about rent or labor. It was about what happens at the table. "Kids just don't drink anymore," he told the hosts. "Something is going to give when you are down 18 percent on beverage sales." A sit-down restaurant makes its margin on the second glass of wine. Gallup now finds that only 54 percent of American adults drink at all — the lowest figure in nearly ninety years of polling. The profit engine of the dining room wasn't the kitchen. It was a drinking culture. And the drinking culture is dissolving.

## The construct moved

Here is the thing the closure headlines miss: none of this is a story about people withdrawing from each other. It is a story about people relocating.

The restaurant, as the twentieth century built it, was a technology for connection. It bundled food, alcohol, courtship, celebration, and belonging into one room, and for three generations it held a near-monopoly on the evening. That monopoly assumed a social contract — that connecting meant sitting, that sitting meant drinking, that the third place had a table in it. The contract has been quietly renegotiated by a generation that is lonelier than any before it and less willing to numb that loneliness with a tab. The Surgeon General has called loneliness an epidemic with the mortality profile of smoking fifteen cigarettes a day; time spent with friends in person collapsed from roughly thirty hours a month in 2003 to ten by 2020, falling hardest among the young. The demand for gathering did not shrink. It intensified.

It just stopped pointing at the dining room. Strava's club count nearly quadrupled to a million; run club participation jumped 59 percent in a single year. Hyrox — a fitness race that barely existed a decade ago — went from 570,000 competitors to 1.5 million in one season, bigger, as its own executives marvel, than all the world marathons combined. Gen Z tells Strava it is four times more likely to want to meet someone at a workout than at a bar, and nearly one in five have gone on a date with someone they met exercising — while Tinder has shed millions of paying users. Pickleball courts, climbing gyms with cafés bolted on, sober-curious meetups growing 92 percent on Eventbrite: the new third places sell the same thing the old ones did — proximity, repetition, a reason to come back — with an activity where the alcohol used to be. Eighty-four percent of people who attend interest-based events say they've made close friends there. That is not a fitness statistic. That is a hospitality statistic wearing running shoes.

## The affordability filter

Affordability is the force that decides what survives the migration. Restaurant prices have outrun grocery prices for years now, household debt sits at a record $18.6 trillion, and nearly half of buy-now-pay-later users paid late this year — up from a third two years ago. When Klarna partnered with DoorDash, the internet recoiled at the idea of financing a burrito in four installments; the joke landed because it was barely a joke. One in five American travelers now plans trips on installment. This is a generation determined to keep living, on borrowed time if necessary.

But watch what they protect. Asked what they would cut first, 44 percent of Americans say dining out. Only 23 percent say fitness. Gym membership just hit an all-time high of 81 million while restaurant traffic fell; Live Nation posted record attendance in the same quarters credit card balances broke records. The pattern is unmistakable: under pressure, spending doesn't retreat toward staying home — it consolidates into experiences that return something durable. A $30 race entry that comes with a community beats a $90 dinner that comes with a receipt. People aren't buying less belonging. They are buying belonging more efficiently.

Hotels tell the same story from the other side. In 2025, American hotel demand fell in a way that, outside a recession, has no modern precedent — but luxury RevPAR rose while economy properties bled, and the fastest-growing thing in lodging is the lifestyle hotel: Marriott building an outdoor collection around cabins, Hilton's experience-driven brands crossing a thousand properties. The middle — the room that is only a room, the dinner that is only a dinner — is what's dying. Chang predicted this barbell for restaurants a decade ago. It has arrived for all of hospitality at once.

## What re-forms

So no, hospitality is not dying. It is re-forming around the truth it forgot: the environment was always the product, and the environment is socially constructed. When the construct was courtship over cocktails, the winning format was the dim room with the good wine list. Now the construct is shared movement, sober mornings, and community you can rejoin every Thursday — and the winning formats are already visible. The run club that ends at a café. The supper club, up 35 percent, where strangers become regulars. The climbing gym designed as a living room. The restaurant that stops selling tables and starts programming belonging — that becomes the venue for the crew rather than the destination for the date.

The operators who wait for the old customer to come back are waiting for a social contract that has already been rewritten. The ones who will define the next twenty years understand what the best hosts have always understood: people never really came for the food. They came for each other. The room that makes that easiest — whatever it serves, however you pay — wins.
