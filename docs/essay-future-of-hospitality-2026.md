# The Great Unbundling of Hospitality

*Run clubs took the crowd. AI took the counter. Fast food falls first.*

*(Alternate titles, ranked: "Run Clubs Are Eating the Restaurant Industry — and AI Is Eating the Rest" · "Nobody Drinks. Everybody Runs." · "The Third Place Sweats Now")*

On a Wednesday night in Manhattan, more than a thousand people gather to run three miles together. They are young, roughly half men and half women, and most of them are not there for the running. Lunge Run Club — like hundreds of clubs that have multiplied across American cities — ends at a bar, but the bar is the afterthought. The point was the miles, the sweat, the easy conversation that happens when you're moving next to a stranger instead of sitting across from one. A few blocks away, a fifteen-year-old restaurant is working its last service. The owner will tell the neighborhood blog what every closing owner now tells the neighborhood blog: the math stopped working. And at the drive-thru up the block, the cheerful voice asking if you'd like to make that a combo is no longer a person.

We are living through the most confusing moment in the modern history of hospitality, because both of these things are true at once. Americans have never spent more on going out, and restaurants have never had a harder time surviving it. To understand why, you have to stop thinking of a restaurant as a place that sells food, and start thinking of it as what it always was: an environment constructed by social culture. Food and beverage is not the product. It is the medium. And the culture that shaped the medium has moved.

## Last call

The numbers describe something closer to a correction than a slump. Twenty-one restaurant chains filed for Chapter 11 in 2024 — the most since the pandemic year itself — and more than twenty followed in 2025, taking Red Lobster, TGI Fridays, and Hooters through the courts. The carnage is not just corporate: independent restaurants shrank 2.3 percent in 2025 while chains grew, and Black Box Intelligence estimates that nine percent of all American full-service restaurants are at risk of closure in 2026. Forty-two percent of operators say they were unprofitable last year. When costs have risen by nearly a third since 2019, losing even a portion of your peak traffic is fatal.

David Chang saw this coming before the data did. He has long described the 2010s restaurant boom as a bubble — "in some degree, it was a bubble for all the restaurants pre-pandemic" — an era when cheap money, Instagram, and a golden age of food media built more dining rooms than the culture could sustain. But his sharpest diagnosis came on the TBPN podcast late last year, and it wasn't about rent or labor. It was about what happens at the table. "Kids just don't drink anymore," he told the hosts. "Something is going to give when you are down 18 percent on beverage sales." A sit-down restaurant makes its margin on the second glass of wine. Gallup now finds that only 54 percent of American adults drink at all — the lowest figure in nearly ninety years of polling. The profit engine of the dining room wasn't the kitchen. It was a drinking culture. And the drinking culture is dissolving.

## The third place sweats now

Here is the thing the closure headlines miss: none of this is a story about people withdrawing from each other. It is a story about people relocating.

The restaurant, as the twentieth century built it, was a technology for connection. It bundled food, alcohol, courtship, celebration, and belonging into one room, and for three generations it held a near-monopoly on the evening. That monopoly assumed a social contract — that connecting meant sitting, that sitting meant drinking, that the third place had a table in it. The contract has been quietly renegotiated by a generation that is lonelier than any before it and less willing to numb that loneliness with a tab. The Surgeon General has called loneliness an epidemic with the mortality profile of smoking fifteen cigarettes a day; time spent with friends in person collapsed from roughly thirty hours a month in 2003 to ten by 2020, falling hardest among the young. The demand for gathering did not shrink. It intensified.

It just stopped pointing at the dining room. Strava's club count nearly quadrupled to a million; run club participation jumped 59 percent in a single year. Hyrox — a fitness race that barely existed a decade ago — went from 570,000 competitors to 1.5 million in one season, bigger, as its own executives marvel, than all the world marathons combined. Gen Z tells Strava it is four times more likely to want to meet someone at a workout than at a bar, and nearly one in five have gone on a date with someone they met exercising — while Tinder has shed millions of paying users. Pickleball courts, climbing gyms with cafés bolted on, sober-curious meetups growing 92 percent on Eventbrite: the new third places sell the same thing the old ones did — proximity, repetition, a reason to come back — with an activity where the alcohol used to be. Eighty-four percent of people who attend interest-based events say they've made close friends there. That is not a fitness statistic. That is a hospitality statistic wearing running shoes.

## The machines take the counter

While the crowd was relocating, a second unbundling arrived from the opposite direction. In 2024, McDonald's shut down its much-hyped IBM drive-thru experiment after viral videos of the AI ringing up hundreds of dollars of unwanted nuggets — then, instead of retreating, signed with Google and came back with a system the company says now completes about ninety percent of orders with no human involved. Wendy's is pushing its Google-built FreshAI past five hundred drive-thrus. Yum Brands runs its AI platform on Nvidia chips across Taco Bell, KFC, and Pizza Hut. Sweetgreen's Infinite Kitchen — a robotic make-line — posted a thirty-one percent restaurant-level margin at its first automated store, roughly ten points above the fleet, with employee turnover forty-five percent lower. The operators' verdict is unanimous even when the pilots embarrass them: the counter is going to the machines.

And fast food will take the hit first and hardest — not because the technology has a taste for burgers, but because of what QSR is. It employs 3.9 million Americans in the most standardized job in the economy. Labor runs about a quarter of sales. Annual turnover exceeds 130 percent — the entire crew replaced more than once a year. Two-thirds to three-quarters of revenue flows through a drive-thru lane, which is to say through a microphone: the easiest interface in hospitality to automate. California's $20 fast-food wage just raised the price of every one of those human hours — economists are still fighting over whether it cost eighteen thousand jobs or none, but no one disputes which direction it pushes an operator's math. Above all: fast food is the one corner of hospitality that never sold hospitality. Nobody idles in a drive-thru lane for belonging. It is pure transaction, and pure transaction is exactly what software eats.

Notice, though, where the machine hits a wall. When Wendy's floated "dynamic pricing" on its new digital menu boards, the backlash was instant and national — Americans accept surge pricing from airlines and Uber, but from a burger chain it read as betrayal. Even fast food carries a faint social contract. Danny Meyer, who built an empire on the distinction — "we're in a hospitality economy, not a service economy" — put ten million dollars into a voice-AI company anyway, on the logic that the machine should take the order so the human can take care of the guest. That is the entire unbundling in a single investment: the transaction goes to the machine, and whatever connection remains becomes the only thing left worth selling.

## Belonging, on installment

Affordability is the force that decides what survives the migration. Restaurant prices have outrun grocery prices for years now, household debt sits at a record $18.6 trillion, and nearly half of buy-now-pay-later users paid late this year — up from a third two years ago. When Klarna partnered with DoorDash, the internet recoiled at the idea of financing a burrito in four installments; the joke landed because it was barely a joke. One in five American travelers now plans trips on installment. This is a generation determined to keep living, on borrowed time if necessary.

But watch what they protect. Asked what they would cut first, 44 percent of Americans say dining out. Only 23 percent say fitness. Gym membership just hit an all-time high of 81 million while restaurant traffic fell; Live Nation posted record attendance in the same quarters credit card balances broke records. The pattern is unmistakable: under pressure, spending doesn't retreat toward staying home — it consolidates into experiences that return something durable. A $30 race entry that comes with a community beats a $90 dinner that comes with a receipt. People aren't buying less belonging. They are buying belonging more efficiently.

Hotels tell the same story from the other side. In 2025, American hotel demand fell in a way that, outside a recession, has no modern precedent — but luxury RevPAR rose while economy properties bled. The machines are moving in there too, pulled by scarcity rather than strategy: hotel employment is still roughly ten percent below pre-pandemic, sixty-five percent of properties report staffing shortages, and seventy percent of travelers now say they'd happily skip the front desk. So the economy segment hands check-in to a kiosk and deletes the night shift, while the top of the market runs the opposite play and makes the human the amenity — Marriott building an outdoor collection around cabins, Hilton's experience-driven brands crossing a thousand properties, service staffed by people whose whole job is to know your name. The middle — the room that is only a room, the dinner that is only a dinner — is what's dying. Chang predicted this barbell for restaurants a decade ago. It has arrived for all of hospitality at once.

## The rebundling

So no, hospitality is not dying. It is unbundling, along one clean line: transaction or connection. Everything transactional — the order, the check-in, the price, the prep line — goes to the machine, and fast food, being almost nothing but transaction, goes first. Everything connective compounds in value, because it is the one thing the model cannot serve. Which returns hospitality to the truth it forgot: the environment was always the product, and the environment is socially constructed. When the construct was courtship over cocktails, the winning format was the dim room with the good wine list. Now the construct is shared movement, sober mornings, and community you can rejoin every Thursday — and the winning formats are already visible. The run club that ends at a café. The supper club, up 35 percent, where strangers become regulars. The climbing gym designed as a living room. The restaurant that lets the software take the order so the room can take care of the people in it.

The operators who wait for the old customer to come back are waiting for a social contract that has already been rewritten. The ones who will define the next twenty years understand what the best hosts have always understood: people never really came for the food. They came for each other. The machines can have everything else. The room that makes connection easiest — whatever it serves, however you pay — wins.

### Accuracy notes (for your own confidence, not for publication)
- **Chang attribution:** the drinking/beverage quotes are verified from his TBPN podcast appearance (Nov 2025). The "bubble" fragment ("it was a bubble for all the restaurants pre-pandemic") is from an earlier podcast appearance, secondhand via D1A's writeup — the essay keeps it un-dated ("he has long described…") so both hold up.
- Counterpoint we chose not to foreground: Datassential found aggregate monthly closures hit a 7-year LOW in April 2025 — the crisis is concentrated (full-service, independents, PE-loaded chains), not uniform. Worth keeping in your back pocket for pushback.
- Hyrox gender split and sellout anecdotes vary by source; the 570K→1.5M figure is SportsPro's (the strongest source).
- **McDonald's "~90% of orders without a human"** is McDonald's own claim from its 2026 convention (5 test stores) — no independent audit; the essay attributes it ("the company says").
- **California $20 wage effects are genuinely contested** — NBER working paper finds ~18k jobs lost; UC Berkeley IRLE finds essentially none. The essay presents both and only claims the direction of the incentive.
- **"Luxury makes the human the amenity"** is an observed strategy divergence (vendor case studies + industry commentary), not a measured staff-per-room dataset. Framed qualitatively on purpose.
- Danny Meyer's widely-quoted "100% of our hearts" AI line traces to a paywalled Axios Pro interview and couldn't be verified verbatim — the essay uses only his verified quotes and the verified $10M ConverseNow investment.
